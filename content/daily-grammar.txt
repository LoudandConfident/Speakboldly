Present simple|Subject + base verb (he/she/it: -s)|Use it for habits and states; it does not always mean “right now.”|She teaches online. / The shop opens at nine.|A timetable can use present simple for the future.
Present continuous|am/is/are + verb-ing|Use it for an activity around now or a temporary situation.|I’m working from home this month.|Temporary does not have to mean happening at this exact second.
Present perfect simple|have/has + past participle|Connect a past event with the present; the result or experience matters.|I’ve lost my keys, so I can’t get in.|With a finished past time, normally use past simple: I lost them yesterday.
Present perfect continuous|have/has + been + verb-ing|Focus on an activity’s duration or recent evidence, not a completed total.|I’ve been reading for an hour. / I’ve read three chapters.|The activity may have just stopped; it need not still be happening.
Past simple|Subject + past form|Place an event or state in a finished past period.|We met last Tuesday.|Questions use did + base verb: Did you meet? Not “Did you met?”
Past continuous|was/were + verb-ing|Show an activity in progress at a past reference point.|I was cooking when you called.|It gives background; it does not by itself say the activity finished.
Past perfect simple|had + past participle|Look back from one past point to an earlier event.|When I arrived, the meeting had ended.|Do not use it for every earlier event when chronological order is already clear.
Past perfect continuous|had + been + verb-ing|Emphasise an activity leading up to a past point.|She had been waiting for an hour when the bus came.|Compare a result: She had written three emails.
Future simple with will|will + base verb|Use will for predictions, offers, promises, and decisions made now.|I’ll carry that bag for you.|Will is a modal, not a signal that every future sentence needs it.
Future continuous|will + be + verb-ing|Imagine an activity in progress at a future point.|At eight tomorrow, I’ll be travelling.|Will you be using the car? can ask about plans without directly requesting a favour.
Future perfect simple|will + have + past participle|View something as completed by a future reference point.|By Friday, I’ll have finished the report.|By sets a deadline; until describes continuation up to a point.
Future perfect continuous|will + have + been + verb-ing|Measure how long an activity has lasted at a future point.|In June, I’ll have been teaching here for five years.|The duration is central; the sentence does not promise the activity will stop then.
Going to or will?|am/is/are going to + base verb / will + base verb|Going to often presents an existing intention or present evidence; will often gives a prediction or new decision.|Look at those clouds—it’s going to rain. / I’ll answer the phone.|These uses overlap; context matters more than a rigid “planned versus unplanned” rule.
Future arrangements|Present continuous + future context|Use a future time to show a personal arrangement.|I’m meeting Salma tomorrow at six.|Compare a timetable: The train leaves at six.
Future time clauses|when/as soon as/until + present form|After these time words, present forms normally refer to the future.|I’ll call when I arrive. / I’ll wait until you’ve finished.|Avoid “when I will arrive” in this kind of time clause.
State verbs|State meaning: usually simple form|Know, own and believe usually describe states, so simple forms are normal.|I know the answer. / I’m thinking about your idea.|Meaning can change the pattern: I think means “I believe”; I’m thinking describes a mental activity.
Used to or be used to?|used to + base verb / be used to + noun or verb-ing|Used to describes a past habit or state; be used to means “be familiar with.”|I used to teach at night. / I’m used to teaching at night.|Get used to describes becoming familiar: I’m getting used to the hours.
Gerunds as subjects|Verb-ing + singular verb|An activity can function as the subject of a sentence.|Reading helps me relax.|Learning two languages takes time: the activity, not “languages,” controls takes.
After a preposition|Preposition + noun or verb-ing|When a verb follows a preposition, normally use its -ing form.|She left without saying goodbye.|To can be a preposition: I look forward to meeting you.
Enjoy, avoid, consider|enjoy/avoid/consider + verb-ing|These verbs normally take an -ing complement, not a to-infinitive.|I enjoy reading. / We considered moving.|Say “suggest doing” or “suggest that we do,” not “suggest to do.”
Decide, hope, agree|decide/hope/agree + to + base verb|These verbs commonly take a to-infinitive.|We agreed to meet early.|Compare agree on + noun or -ing: We agreed on meeting at six.
Stop doing or stop to do?|stop + verb-ing / stop + to + base verb|Stop doing ends that activity; stop to do interrupts something else for a purpose.|She stopped talking. / She stopped to answer the phone.|One ends talking; the other pauses another activity in order to answer.
Remember doing or remember to do?|remember + verb-ing / remember + to + base verb|Remember doing recalls a past event; remember to do means not forgetting a required action.|I remember locking it. / Remember to lock it.|The first is a memory; the second is a reminder.
Try doing or try to do?|try + verb-ing / try + to + base verb|Try doing tests a possible solution; try to do makes an effort.|Try restarting it. / I tried to lift it, but it was too heavy.|A test and an effort can overlap, but the focus is different.
Regret doing or regret to say?|regret + verb-ing / regret + to inform/say|Regret doing looks back with sadness; regret to inform introduces unwelcome news formally.|I regret sending that email. / We regret to inform you that it is cancelled.|The infinitive pattern is common with announcements, not interchangeable with every regret.
Go on doing or go on to do?|go on + verb-ing / go on + to + base verb|Go on doing continues the same activity; go on to do introduces a later, different activity.|She went on explaining. / She went on to lead the team.|The second can describe a later achievement, not just the next minute.
Let, make and help|let/make + object + base verb; help + object + (to) base verb|Let and active make omit to; help accepts either form.|Let me explain. / She helped me (to) prepare.|In the passive, make regains to: We were made to wait.
Prefer or would rather?|prefer + noun/verb-ing + to + noun/verb-ing; would rather + base verb|Prefer compares general choices; would rather often expresses a choice in context.|I prefer walking to driving. / I’d rather walk today.|Would rather can also describe a general preference; the verb pattern is the key distinction.
For or since?|for + duration / since + starting point|For measures length; since locates the beginning.|I’ve lived here for five years, since 2021.|For also works with finished periods: I lived there for five years.
During or while?|during + noun phrase / while + clause|During names a period; while introduces what was happening.|During the meeting, I took notes. / While she spoke, I took notes.|While can also take a reduced -ing clause: while waiting.
By or until?|by + deadline / until + endpoint|By means “no later than”; until means something continues up to that time.|Send it by Friday. / I’m staying until Friday.|Not until highlights a delayed start: I won’t leave until Friday.
Already, yet and still|already: earlier than expected; yet: up to now; still: continuing|These words describe different expectations, not different tenses.|I’ve already eaten. / Have you eaten yet? / I’m still eating.|Still haven’t emphasises an expected action remaining undone.
Been or gone?|have/has been to / have/has gone to|Been to normally suggests a completed visit; gone to suggests being away now.|Maya has been to Rome. / Maya has gone to Rome.|Has been in Rome for a week describes a continuing stay, not a completed visit.
Because or because of?|because + clause / because of + noun phrase|The reason can be expressed as a complete clause or a noun phrase.|We stayed in because it rained. / We stayed in because of the rain.|Because of it was raining mixes the two patterns.
Although or despite?|although + clause / despite + noun or verb-ing|Both introduce a contrast, but the grammar after them differs.|Although it rained, we went out. / Despite the rain, we went out.|Despite has no of. In spite of keeps all three words.
Unless or if not?|unless + clause|Unless means “except if” and often replaces an if-not condition.|We’ll leave unless it rains. / We’ll leave if it doesn’t rain.|Check the logic before replacing if not; unless is not a mechanical substitute in every context.
Even if or even though?|even if: hypothetical condition / even though: accepted fact|One says the condition would not change the result; the other contrasts with a fact.|I’ll go even if it rains. / I went even though it rained.|The first does not tell us whether rain will actually happen.
If or whether?|whether: alternatives / if: condition or some indirect yes-no questions|Whether makes alternatives explicit and is needed before a to-infinitive.|I don’t know whether to stay. / I wonder if she’s home.|After a preposition, use whether: It depends on whether she agrees.
Less or fewer?|fewer + plural count noun / less + uncountable noun|Use fewer for separate countable items and less for amounts.|Fewer emails, less stress.|Less is normal for measures treated as one amount: less than ten minutes.
Much, many and a lot of|many + plural count noun / much + uncountable noun|A lot of works with both, especially in affirmative everyday sentences.|Many questions; much information; a lot of both.|Information is uncountable: two pieces of information, not “two informations.”
Few or a few?|few: hardly any / a few: some|The article changes the emphasis from shortage to availability.|Few people replied. / A few people replied, so we can begin.|Little and a little make the same contrast with uncountable nouns.
Each or every?|each/every + singular count noun|Each highlights individuals; every presents the whole set.|Each learner has a goal. / Every learner needs practice.|Each can stand alone: Each has a goal. Every normally needs a noun or a compound such as everyone.
Another, other and the other|another + singular noun / other + plural or uncountable noun|Another means an additional or different one; the other identifies the remaining one in a known pair.|Another idea; other ideas; one pen and the other pen.|Others replaces a plural noun: Some agreed; others didn’t.
Say or tell?|say + words / tell + person + message|Tell normally needs the listener when reporting a message; say does not.|She said she was busy. / She told me she was busy.|Tell a story and tell the truth are common exceptions to the listener pattern.
Borrow or lend?|borrow something from someone / lend something to someone|Borrow receives temporarily; lend gives temporarily.|Can I borrow your pen? / Can you lend me your pen?|The same exchange has two perspectives: I borrow; you lend.
Mustn’t or don’t have to?|must not: prohibition / do not have to: no obligation|The negative forms have very different meanings.|You mustn’t park here. / You don’t have to drive.|No obligation still allows a choice; prohibition does not.
Must have or had to?|must have + past participle / had to + base verb|Must have usually makes a deduction; had to describes past necessity.|She must have left. / She had to leave early.|For a negative deduction, use can’t have: She can’t have seen it.
Should have|should have + past participle|Look back on an action as advisable, expected, or regrettably missed.|I should have checked. / The parcel should have arrived by now.|The second can express expectation rather than criticism.
Zero and first conditionals|if + present, present / if + present, will + base verb|Zero conditionals describe regular results; first conditionals concern a real future possibility.|If I skip lunch, I get hungry. / If I finish early, I’ll call.|These are useful patterns, not bans on other modals or imperative results.
Second conditional|if + past form, would + base verb|Use past form to create hypothetical distance, not necessarily past time.|If I had more time, I would travel.|If I were you is standard hypothetical advice; was is also common with I/he/she in informal contexts.
Third conditional|if + had + past participle, would have + past participle|Imagine a different past condition and its past result.|If I had left earlier, I would have caught the train.|It imagines an alternative; it cannot change what happened.
Mixed conditional|if + past perfect, would + base verb|Connect an unreal past cause with an unreal present result.|If I had studied medicine, I would be a doctor now.|Match the forms to the times you mean, rather than memorising one fixed mixed pattern.
Relative clauses: who or which?|who: people / which: things / that: either in defining clauses|A defining clause identifies which person or thing you mean.|The learner who called is here. / My laptop, which is new, is broken.|After a comma in a non-defining clause, use who or which, not that.
A, the or no article?|a/an: one non-identified item / the: identified item|Article choice depends on what the listener can identify, not simply whether a noun appeared earlier.|Please close the door. / I need a pen.|A shared situation can identify the door on its very first mention.
Passive voice|be + past participle|Make the receiver of an action the subject when that is the useful focus.|The report was sent yesterday.|A passive does not always need a by-phrase; the agent may be unknown or unimportant.
Reported speech|reporting verb + clause|Backshift often reflects a past viewpoint; it is not compulsory when a fact remains current.|She said she was tired. / He said the Earth is round.|Pronouns and time expressions change only when the speaker’s viewpoint requires it.
Wish and hope|wish + past/past perfect / hope + clause|Wish often imagines a different reality; hope expresses a desired possibility.|I wish I knew. / I hope I pass.|Wish + would often asks for a change: I wish the neighbours would be quieter.
So or such?|so + adjective/adverb / such + noun phrase|Both intensify, but attach to different kinds of phrase.|It was so useful. / It was such a useful lesson.|With many, much, few and little (amount), use so: so many ideas.
Too or enough?|too + adjective / adjective + enough|Too signals a problematic excess; enough signals sufficient degree.|It’s too hot to drink. / It’s cool enough to drink.|Very hot intensifies without necessarily saying the drink cannot be drunk.
Only after: inversion|Only after + phrase/clause + auxiliary + subject + verb|An initial restrictive phrase can trigger inversion in the main clause.|Only after the call did I understand.|The inversion belongs to the main clause: Only after she explained did I understand.
