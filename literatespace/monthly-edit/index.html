THE MONTHLY EDIT · NOVEMBER 2026

One month.
Many ways to belong.

A little more of the world, every month.

This month, we follow the stories behind the celebrations: the people we remember, the light we share, and the questions that make a familiar world feel wider.

A five-minute read · for learners and non-learners alike

Open a calendar and you see dates. Look a little closer and you find families, memories, beliefs and ways of seeing the world. November has offered plenty of reasons to do that. Across different cultures, people have been remembering loved ones, lighting lamps, asking difficult questions and gathering around tables. These moments are not interchangeable, and no tradition tells the whole story of the people who celebrate it. But each can open a door into a life that looks different from our own.

In Mexico, the end of October and beginning of November bring Día de los Muertos. In the Indigenous communities described by UNESCO’s account of the tradition, families prepare offerings for relatives and loved ones who have died. Flowers, food and familiar objects help give remembrance a visible place in everyday life. Seen only through photographs, the colours can dominate the story. Looking beyond them reveals something more personal: the wish to keep a relationship present through the details that made somebody themselves. Even an ordinary favourite dish can hold a history that a photograph alone cannot explain.

On 8 November, the date listed in India’s 2026 holiday calendar, many communities marked Diwali, or Deepavali. Lamps, time with family and friends, and decorative patterns are among the traditions explored by the Smithsonian’s National Museum of Asian Art. The celebrations extend beyond a single day, and their meaning varies between communities. That variety matters. The phrase “festival of lights” offers a beginning, but it cannot contain every household’s rituals, memories or reasons for gathering. A more interesting story begins when we ask what a celebration means to the people taking part.

The month also made room for a different kind of gathering. World Philosophy Day fell on 19 November. UNESCO’s 2026 programme in Paris centred on living together in a world of shared challenges, bringing philosophical discussion into conversations about climate, migration, health and technology. Philosophy can sound like something that belongs on a distant bookshelf. Yet its questions are close to daily life: What do we owe one another? Who is missing from a conversation? What would make us reconsider a belief? There is something refreshing about a public occasion that makes space for questions rather than demanding an immediate answer.

In the United States, Thanksgiving fell on 26 November, as shown in the federal holiday calendar. For many people it is an opportunity to gather and give thanks. It also carries histories that deserve more attention than a familiar holiday image can provide. A 24 November feature from the National Museum of the American Indian shares varied Native perspectives on the day, including remembrance, community, family and generosity. Reading those voices adds depth to the conversation. Gratitude becomes more thoughtful when it leaves room for another person’s experience, including the parts that complicate the story we already know.

What connects these moments is not a single message or a universal way to celebrate. It is the chance to notice how much lies behind a date, an image or a word. We can enjoy a tradition’s beauty while learning its context. We can discover a new expression and remain interested in the person who uses it. We can ask about an unfamiliar custom without expecting somebody to represent an entire culture. As November gives way to December, that feels like something worth carrying with us: a little less certainty that we already understand, and a little more curiosity about the world beyond our own experience.

More language. Way more perspective.
